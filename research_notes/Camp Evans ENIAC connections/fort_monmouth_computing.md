# Fort Monmouth / Camp Evans computing 1946-1957 and ENIAC lineage: testing the four InfoAge claims

Site key: "Camp Evans" = Evans Signal Laboratory / Evans Area, Wall NJ (now InfoAge). "Fort Monmouth" = main post, Oceanport/Eatontown NJ (Signal Corps Engineering Laboratories, later Signal Research & Development Lab). Contractor = Sylvania, Moore School, etc.
Source quality scale: A primary/official, B reputable secondary, C hobbyist/museum self-description, D unverified/mailing list.

## Claim 1: Campus "part of cutting-edge computer research from about 1941-1957"

### Takeaway
Camp Evans radar work in 1941-45 is documented as one stimulus for Mauchly's ENIAC thinking (via Mauchly's own oral history as relayed by InfoAge). The Army's actual computer programs (Fieldata, MOBIDIC) were run from Fort Monmouth, 1955-62, and I found no independent source placing core computer research at Camp Evans specifically. The claim is loosely worded and unsourced on the InfoAge page.

### Cited Findings
- InfoAge's page makes the four claims with no sources or evidence given on the page. — [InfoAge Computer Research](https://www.infoage.org/history-ia/computer-research/) (C, the claimant)
- InfoAge's ENIAC article quotes Mauchly's Smithsonian oral histories: he worked on a Signal Corps project from New Jersey calculating radar antenna radiation patterns, correlated with Camp Evans measurements; hand calculation was the bottleneck. — [InfoAge ENIAC/Camp Evans article](https://infoage.org/eniac-development-impelled-by-camp-evans-project/) (C relaying primary; cites Smithsonian AC0196 mauc730110/mauc730206, which I did not read directly)
- The article itself does not mention screens, mobile computers or ASCII. — same source
- Camp Evans history per Koblentz: Marconi station, Navy WWI, Army Signal Corps R&D lab during/after WWII, closed late 1990s; he said its computer-development role was "severely understated." No detail given. — [SIGCIS post, 2009](https://lists.sigcis.org/archives/list/members@lists.sigcis.org/thread/T5WUSQOF3RKB5XI3MLAZRAAE7XID3QZG/) (D/C, advocacy by InfoAge volunteer)
- The Signal Corps Engineering Laboratories were at Fort Monmouth, consolidated in the Signal Corps Center in 1949. — [Wikipedia: Signal Corps Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_Corps_Laboratories) (B-, pointer only)

### Inferences
- "1941" matches the radar-antenna period Mauchly describes, not any computing program. "1957" has no obvious anchor at Camp Evans; Fieldata started 1955 and MOBIDIC contract 1956, both Fort Monmouth-managed.
- The claim is defensible only as "Signal Corps radar/electronics site whose problems helped motivate ENIAC", not as a computer-research campus.

### Gaps
- No primary document (SCEL/Evans lab report, org chart) found showing which computing groups sat at Camp Evans vs the main post.
- Mauchly transcripts not read directly.

## Claim 2: Projects "loaned technology to the first computers that had output screens"

### Takeaway
No source supports this as worded. The only specific version found is a VCF page claim that Camp Evans engineers demonstrated CRT technology in 1953 to IBM and MIT teams, tied to Whirlwind/SAGE; it is unsourced. Whirlwind (MIT, operational 1951) had CRT displays before 1953, and Williams-tube machines (Manchester 1948; Princeton IAS, ORDVAC, etc.) displayed memory on CRTs, so "first computers with output screens" is not supportable.

### Cited Findings
- VCF page: Camp Evans engineers demonstrated CRT technology in 1953 to IBM and MIT teams, who then collaborated on Whirlwind II/SAGE, which it calls "the first computer using CRT output"; no speaker or source given. — [VCF, Computer history in NJ](https://vcfed.org/?p=291) (C/D)
- InfoAge's own ENIAC page does not mention screens. — [InfoAge ENIAC article](https://infoage.org/eniac-development-impelled-by-camp-evans-project/)

### Inferences
- Whirlwind and Williams-tube chronology (from general knowledge, not fetched here) predates 1953, so "first" fails. A "radar display expertise informed SAGE" claim is plausible (SAGE ingested radar data) but I found no document for a Camp Evans demonstration.

### Gaps
- Could not verify the 1953 demonstration, who attended, or any Whirlwind/SAGE document naming Evans Signal Lab. Whirlwind/Williams-tube dates not sourced in this pass; verify before publishing.

## Claim 3: "earliest non-prototype mobile computers"

### Takeaway
Best match is MOBIDIC (Sylvania, for the Signal Corps, managed from Fort Monmouth). It was real, trailer-mounted and fielded, but its dates are 1956 contract, 1959-60 delivery, and the "Camp Evans designed it" attribution rests on a hobbyist source. "Earliest" is contested by DYSEAC (NBS, 1953-54, vans) and "non-prototype" is arguable since only a handful were built.

### Cited Findings
- Fieldata ran 1955-1962 under the Signal Research and Development Laboratory at Fort Monmouth; cut short in 1962. — [U.S. Army, CECOM historian](https://www.army.mil/article/291122/early_army_mobile_computing_development_in_the_signal_corps) (A/B)
- MOBIDIC unveiled 1960 as Army's "first large-scale mobile computer"; first operational unit to Seventh Army, Europe; 30-foot trailer; managed 7th Army repair parts; later commercialized. Article does not mention Camp Evans or Sylvania. — same
- Sylvania won the contract Sept 1956; MOBIDIC A delivered to Fort Monmouth Dec 1959; Army designation AN/MYK-1; commercial S 9400; builds A, B, C; counts differ. — [Wikipedia: MOBIDIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOBIDIC) (B-, via search summary only; page not fetched)
- Computer History Museum object record lists 1958 for MOBIDIC model. — [CHM](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/mobile-computing/18/315/1673) (B, from search summary)
- VCF page: Camp Evans built MOBIDIC in 1956 in two 30-foot trailers; "perhaps the first mobile computer that was more than just a one-time prototype"; cites an unnamed VCF East lecture. — [VCF](https://vcfed.org/?p=291) (C); a VCF mailing-list post similarly says "designed by the staff of Camp Evans" and six units built — [VCF Mid-Atlantic list](https://lists.vcfed.org/archives/list/vcf-midatlantic@lists.vcfed.org/thread/RPXZSYXOF6Y3V2GAKSCJHQMIX4GI4H4A/) (D)
- DYSEAC: NBS-built mobile computer (1953-54) is often cited as earlier; a SIGCIS poster disputes a textbook placing it at Fort Monmouth, saying it was NBS-built and tested at White Sands. — [SIGCIS thread](https://lists.sigcis.org/archives/list/members@lists.sigcis.org/thread/4Z4TGLWORM2BI55FYIDKQJ5F4BUHEGBV/) (D); DYSEAC via [Wikipedia: MOBIDIC summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOBIDIC)

### Inferences
- Attribution of the MOBIDIC contract/program to Fort Monmouth (SRDL) is well supported; attribution to Camp Evans as the design site is only supported by museum-affiliated sources and conflicts in emphasis with the Army historian. Sylvania (Needham MA) did the design/build.
- "Non-prototype" is defensible only for MOBIDIC B/C and the S 9400 production; "earliest" is better as "first large-scale / first fielded Army mobile computer".

### Gaps
- Could not confirm whether any MOBIDIC work occurred in the Evans Area; the Army article is silent. Wikipedia page not fetched directly (fetch blocked); figures come via search summaries.

## Claim 4: "directly led to ASCII"

### Takeaway
Fieldata, a Signal Corps program run from Fort Monmouth, is credited by several sources as a major influence on ASCII, but "directly led to" overstates it: ASCII was developed by the ASA X3.2 committee, was well advanced as Fieldata deployed, and borrowed structural ideas (columns, control placement). Camp Evans specifically is not documented as the Fieldata developer.

### Cited Findings
- Fieldata character code described as a late-1950s Signal Corps project, preceded by ITA2 and succeeded by ASCII, "huge influence" on ASCII. Wikipedia dates project from 1956 [citation needed] to 1962. — [Wikipedia: Fieldata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fieldata) (B-, via search summary)
- Fieldata used 1957 into early 1960s with MOBIDIC and Philco machines, no commercial use; "enormous influence on the design of ASCII"; ASCII design already well advanced when Fieldata deployed; W. F. Leubbert, author of the Fieldata reference article, served on the ASCII committee; ASCII kept 4-bit table structure. — [Jennings/sr-ix character code history](https://www.sr-ix.com/Archive/CharCodeHist/) (B; only first ~100k of 145k characters read; page did not mention X3.2, Bemer or Mackenzie in that portion)
- Mackenzie's 1980 book is the standard reference — [PDF copy](https://hcs64.com/files/Mackenzie%20-%20Coded%20Character%20Sets%20History%20and%20Development.pdf) (A, located but NOT read)
- Bemer/Smith/Williams, "An improved data processing code," CACM May 1961, exists as a pre-ASCII proposal — [Ed Thelen copy](https://ed-thelen.org/comp-hist/ImprovedDataProcessingCode_BemerSmithWilliams_CACM_May1961.pdf) (A, located but NOT read)
- Fieldata program: standards for codes, common instruction sets, interfaces, under SRDL Fort Monmouth, 1955-62. — [U.S. Army](https://www.army.mil/article/291122/early_army_mobile_computing_development_in_the_signal_corps)
- VCF claims Camp Evans "made Fieldata, which was the basis for ASCII"; no source. — [VCF](https://vcfed.org/?p=291) (C/D)

### Inferences
- Defensible rewording: "Fieldata, developed by the Signal Corps at Fort Monmouth in 1955-62, was one of the principal influences on ASCII (X3.4-1963)." Not "directly led to".
- X3.4-1963 date is from general knowledge; not confirmed from a fetched source here.

### Gaps
- Not verified from Mackenzie or Bemer text: X3.2 committee records, who from Signal Corps sat on it, and the degree of Fieldata influence. Bemer's own account not read. Fieldata inventors by name not identified (only Leubbert as an author).
- No source distinguishing a Camp Evans group from the main-post lab on Fieldata.

## ENIAC lineage: did Fort Monmouth/Camp Evans use or sponsor ENIAC-family machines or employ alumni?

### Takeaway
Documented: ENIAC/EDVAC were Aberdeen/BRL (Ordnance) projects; the Army Signal Corps sponsored a Moore School machine (MSAC, 1950-53, unfinished) and Mauchly worked a Signal Corps radar-antenna contract in the early 1940s. I found no evidence that Fort Monmouth or Camp Evans operated ENIAC, EDVAC, ORDVAC or a UNIVAC in 1946-57, nor of a named ENIAC alumni roster there.

### Cited Findings
- ENIAC and EDVAC were developed for the Ballistics Research Laboratory at Aberdeen Proving Ground. — [U.S. Army](https://www.army.mil/article/291122/early_army_mobile_computing_development_in_the_signal_corps)
- MSAC built for Army Signal Corps in 1951 per Hagley; Penn archives say designed from 1950; work stopped ~85% complete Nov 1953 per a Moore School report cited on SIGCIS; a 1950 ONR bulletin (poor OCR) says SCEL sponsored component research and the Ordnance Dept. was the sponsor, so sponsorship is conflicted. — [Hagley](https://findingaids.hagley.org/repositories/3/resources/1853); [Penn Archives](https://archives.upenn.edu/?p=2646); [SIGCIS](https://lists.sigcis.org/archives/list/members@lists.sigcis.org/thread/4Z4TGLWORM2BI55FYIDKQJ5F4BUHEGBV/); [DTIC AD0694599](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD0694599.pdf) (not directly readable; 403)
- Mauchly's Signal Corps antenna-pattern work from NJ is the Camp Evans link. — [InfoAge ENIAC article](https://infoage.org/eniac-development-impelled-by-camp-evans-project/)

### Inferences
- The strongest honest Camp Evans-ENIAC statement is "a Signal Corps radar problem at Camp Evans was one input to Mauchly's motivation" (Mauchly's recollection, 1973), not that Evans contributed technology.

### Gaps
- No evidence found on SCEL use of BRLESC, ORDVAC, UNIVAC; no SCEL staff-with-ENIAC-background list. Not searched: SCEL annual reports, Monmouth Message, CBI holdings, IEEE Annals. Mackenzie, Bemer, MSAC DTIC report were not read. Whirlwind/SAGE CRT chronology unsourced here.
