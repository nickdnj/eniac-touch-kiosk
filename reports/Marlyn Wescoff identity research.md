# Mauchly's "Marlene Wescott" is very likely Marlyn Wescoff

**Very likely yes, but not proven by any direct source.** In his 10 January 1973 oral history, John Mauchly names a "Marlene Wescott" as one of his hired computers on the Moore School's Signal Corps antenna contract and places her among the six women who programmed ENIAC. No other source uses that spelling or first name for anyone at the Moore School, and every other source on the six names Marlyn Wescoff (later Meltzer). The identification rests on several independent matches: her slot in Mauchly's list of the six, a near-identical first name, a husband "Phil" who was a dentist (Dr. Philip Meltzer, a dentist), a Temple 1942 social-studies/secondary-education degree that fits "trained to be a teacher of high school history," and the same career path (Moore School computer, then numerical integration, then ENIAC). No document found contradicts the match, and no second candidate has surfaced. The weak points are that the dentist evidence is tertiary and user-transcribed, and that her own account of her first job (weather calculations in the biographies, versus Signal Corps integrations in Mauchly's) has not been reconciled. A kiosk may say that Mauchly recalled hiring a woman who matches Wescoff; it should not state flatly that she worked on the antenna contract. This report stands on the notes in the research folder and the primary transcript; the earlier Camp Evans note flagged this same lead and asked for verification, which is what follows.

## Mauchly's passage is primary, specific, and fallible

The transcript is the Smithsonian's NMAH AC0196 interview of Mauchly by Henry Tropp ([Smithsonian transcript](https://www.si.edu/media/NMAH/NMAH-AC0196_mauc730110.pdf)). On PDF pp. 68-71 Mauchly walks through the women who became ENIAC's first programmers. He names Frances Bilas and Kathleen McNulty (p. 68), Betty Snyder and then "Marlene Wescott" (p. 69), and Ruth Lichterman (p. 70). He then adds Betty Jennings as "a sixth girl" (p. 71), after Tropp raises Adele Goldstine, who was not one of the six. That is six names, and "Wescoff" appears nowhere in the transcript; the only match for "Wesco*" is this passage.

About Wescott he says several concrete things. She married a dentist named Phil, whose last name he cannot recall. She trained as a high school history teacher, with a history major at a school he does not remember. He hired her "as one of my computers on this Signal Corps project" to do step-by-step integrations, and he does not know how she came to the school or who recommended her. She "never made a mistake," worked "like an automaton," and had no interest in mathematics. After being transferred to the numerical integration work and doing well, she volunteered and was accepted by Goldstine, apparently, for ENIAC.

The same transcript supplies the project context. Grist Brainerd arranged a contract with "the Signal Corps in New Jersey" to study a parabolic reflector with a dipole at its focus, and the method was numerical integration. Mauchly had not finished those computations when ENIAC began, and he completed some in early 1943. The staff he names is small: Brainerd, Burks and Sharpless on the experimental side, Witmer and an unnamed physics professor as advisors, and desk-calculator "computers," of whom only Wescott is named. He does not name Camp Evans, and he never says "Ballistics Research Laboratory" for her.

This is a primary source, but it is a recollection from some thirty years later with admitted gaps. Mauchly himself concedes he forgot the husband's surname and the school. He also does not quite place her on the antenna job at a specific date; "hired her for the Signal Corps project" is as exact as it gets.

## Her documented biography fits in every checkable respect

Born 2 March 1922 in Philadelphia, she died in Yardley, Pennsylvania, in December 2008 ([Find a Grave, Marlyn Meltzer](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/211908992/marlyn-meltzer)). Temple University awarded her degree in 1942 (Wikipedia, ETHW and Find a Grave all agree). The University of Pennsylvania's own news office says the degree was in social studies ([Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/worlds-first-general-purpose-computer-turns-75)). A 2011 AAUW-Illinois newsletter, which cites W. Barkley Fritz's interview-based 1996 article, says it came from Temple's Secondary Education Department and that a friend told her the Moore School was hiring ([AAUW-IL newsletter](https://aauw-il.aauw.net/files/2013/04/May11.pdf)). That newsletter misspells her first name as "Mary," so it is a weak vessel for a strong claim.

Those facts fit Mauchly's "history major, trained to teach high school history" without confirming it. No retrieved source says "history" or "teacher" for her. One Spanish-language wiki profile claims a mathematics major with a business-machines minor, but its only listed source (ETHW) does not say so, so that claim is unsupported ([EcuRed](https://www.ecured.cu/Marlyn_Wescoff_Meltzer)). A master's degree in English and a 1967 editing career that surfaced in one search summary belong to Jean Bartik and should be discarded.

On marriage, WikiTree, as relayed by a search summary, records her marrying Philip Meltzer in Philadelphia on 2 February 1947 ([WikiTree](https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Wescoff-1)). Wikipedia and ETHW say she left the ENIAC team in 1947 to marry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlyn_Meltzer); [ETHW](https://ethw.org/Marlyn_Meltzer)). The couple had a son, Hugh, and a daughter, Joy. Her later life included Meals on Wheels, library volunteering, and induction into the WITI Hall of Fame in 1997.

## The dentist evidence is real but tertiary

The earlier husband-profession note, which said no source states his job, was wrong. Find a Grave memorial 280641824 for Philip Meltzer (16 October 1918 to 10 November 1997) transcribes an obituary calling him a dentist, an Army captain, a 1948 graduate of Temple's dental school, and a member of the American, New Jersey and Mercer County dental associations ([Find a Grave, Philip Meltzer](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/280641824)). It names his wife as Marlyn Wescoff Meltzer. Her own memorial links "Dr. Philip Meltzer" as spouse but does not itself say dentist.

This should be rated honestly. It is tertiary: a volunteer's transcription of a newspaper obituary, not the obituary itself and not a licensure record. The research fetch returned a paraphrase rather than verbatim text, so exact wording was not captured. The obituary has internal discrepancies. It gives his age as 69 although a 1918 birth makes him 79, and her memorial shows a death date of 4 December 2008 while its obituary text says "Thursday, December 7, 2008." Neither date fits that weekday, since 7 December 2008 was a Sunday and 4 December was a Thursday, so 4 December is the likelier date; that is a calendar inference, not a sourced fact. Those errors do not touch the profession, but they show the transcription is not clean.

The detail is still useful because it is independent of Mauchly. The man who was a dentist, first name Phil, married Marlyn Wescoff, and Mauchly independently recalled "a dentist by the name of Phil." The 1947 marriage precedes his 1948 dental degree, so Mauchly's wording describes the husband as he was in 1973, not in 1947; that is no contradiction. The chance that a different Moore School woman had a husband named Phil who was a dentist and also a history teacher background is small, but a record of the obituary itself would be needed to move this from "strong" to "documented."

## Sources disagree about her first job, and one resolution is plausible but undocumented

The conflict is about what she did before ENIAC. Mauchly says Signal Corps antenna integrations by desk calculator under his own project, followed by a transfer to numerical integration. ETHW, Wikipedia and I-Programmer say she was hired by the Moore School in 1942 for weather calculations because she could run an adding machine, and in 1943 moved to ballistic trajectory calculations among roughly eighty women computers ([ETHW](https://ethw.org/Marlyn_Meltzer); [I-Programmer](https://www.i-programmer.info/history/9-machines/17963-eniacs-women-programmers-.html?start=3)). Penn Today adds that her weather unit was disbanded and she was encouraged to apply for civil service to stay on ballistics work. The ETHW, Wikipedia and I-Programmer accounts form one lineage, not three independent confirmations, because ETHW carries no references. Light's peer-reviewed article adds that BRL ran one desk-calculator group and one differential-analyzer group, from which Goldstine chose six who reported to Holberton, and it lists Wescoff as reporting in July 1945 ([Light, Technology and Culture](https://cs.brown.edu/courses/cs1951i/lightWhenComputersWereWomen.pdf)).

These accounts do not collide on the endpoint, only on the middle. Everyone agrees on the arc: a desk-calculator hire in 1942, a move to trajectory integration, and selection for ENIAC in 1945. The disagreement is whether the first assignment was weather (biographies) or Signal Corps antenna work (Mauchly). The most economical reading is that Mauchly's 1942 Signal Corps project and the "weather calculations" describe the same early period viewed from different sides, with the biographies carrying a label that may be a garble. That reading is a hypothesis, nothing more: no source ties Wescoff to Mauchly or to the antenna contract besides Mauchly's own passage, and no source says outright that the weather work was a Signal Corps contract.

Two further tensions resolve more easily. Mauchly says he hired her, while accounts of ENIAC selection credit Goldstine; these are different events (hiring in 1942 versus selection for ENIAC in 1945), and Mauchly himself credits Goldstine for the ENIAC move. The AAUW account says a friend told her the Moore School was hiring, while Mauchly says he does not know who recommended her; both can be true because he was not the person who recruited her. The name conflict, "Marlene Wescott" versus "Marlyn Wescoff," is best read as spoken-word drift and transcription of a name he half remembered, and the transcript itself shows him groping for names. Rare variants such as "Wescott," "Westcott" and "Marilyn" do appear in sources, but none uses "Marlene" for her.

## What is primary, circumstantial, and unverified

The primary evidence is Mauchly's passage itself. Everything that connects it to Wescoff is circumstantial, though the circumstances converge. Her placement in the list of six, with the other five names fully accounted for, is a structural match that leaves essentially no room for another person. The first-name similarity, the dentist husband named Phil, and the history-teacher background are three independent personal details that all line up with a documented woman, and none of them is contradicted anywhere. A coincidence would require a second woman at the Moore School with a similar name, a dentist husband called Phil, and a history-teacher background who also became an ENIAC programmer, and searches of the Smithsonian finding-aid abstracts (e-g, i-m, s-z) and the standard histories found no such person.

What remains unverified is substantial. Nothing in her own words has been read, because Fritz 1996, Kleiman's 2022 *Proving Ground*, and the ENIAC Programmers Project interviews were not accessible. No contemporaneous personnel record, payroll, contract roster or Penn archive item names her on the Signal Corps project. The dentist's obituary has not been seen verbatim. Temple's registrar and the 1942 yearbook have not been checked for her major, and the Penn Almanac "social studies" statement reached the research only via a search summary. Abstracts a-d and n-r of the AC0196 finding aids were not searched, and other full-length AC0196 transcripts (Eckert, Burks, Goldstine, Brainerd, Sharpless, Mauchly's later sessions) were not read.

## Conclusion

The honest rating is "very likely," not "established." The convergence of independent personal details makes a different woman implausible, so a museum can proceed on the identification with a hedge. The real open question has shifted from "who is Wescott" to "what was Wescoff's first job," and the answer would also say whether the weather label is a mistake, a different project, or a cover for the antenna contract. That question matters more for the exhibit than the name, since it determines whether a visitor can be told she began on the radar-antenna calculations that pushed Mauchly toward ENIAC.

One document would settle it: her own interview account of her first assignment (Fritz 1996 or the Kleiman recordings), or a Moore School personnel or payroll record naming her under the Signal Corps contract. A single sentence from her saying Mauchly hired her, or an entry listing her on the project, would convert the inference to a documented fact.

## Safe wording for the kiosk

Safe, and supported: "Marlyn Wescoff graduated from Temple University in 1942 and joined the Moore School as a human computer, working on calculations by hand and desk calculator. In 1945 she was one of the first six ENIAC programmers." Also safe: "John Mauchly recalled hiring a young woman named Marlene Wescott as a computer on the school's Signal Corps radar-antenna project; she later moved to ENIAC. Historians believe he meant Marlyn Wescoff, whose husband was a dentist." If space allows, the second sentence should keep the attribution to Mauchly's memory and the word "believe."

Avoid: stating as fact that she worked on the antenna contract; saying Mauchly "named her" without noting the different spelling; calling her a mathematics major; naming her husband's profession without attribution to an obituary; stating the date of her death as 7 December without noting the 4 December discrepancy; and saying Camp Evans was the sponsor of her work, since Mauchly says only "the Signal Corps in New Jersey."
