**Subject:** A fact-check pass on ENIAC worth having on the floor

Hey [name],

I put together an episode on ENIAC and ended up running most of the
standard facts back to their original sources — the 1946 Report on the
ENIAC, the Maintenance Manual, the BRL memos, the war department press
release. A fair number of things I'd always said on the floor turned out
to be wrong, or at least not traceable to anything. I made a one-page fact
card out of the pass and thought it'd be useful to you regardless of
whether you ever listen to the episode.

A few that changed how I'll answer questions:

- "They never turned the machine off" isn't in any contemporaneous source.
  The 1946 documentation actually describes a stop switch, an instruction
  to power down unused units' heaters, and a heater switch plus elapsed-hour
  meter on every one of the 40 panels — they were managing tube-hours
  panel by panel, not leaving it running.
- "A tube failed about every two days" is Eckert's own memory, given in an
  interview in 1989 — 43 years after the fact. The Army's own 1955 survey
  gives a mean error-free run of 5.6 hours and an operating ratio of 0.69.
  Different claim, worth knowing which one you're citing.
- "The Army demanded six firing tables a day" is a misquote. The 1944 memo
  says six *requests for new tables* a day — a word dropped somewhere in
  the 1990s and copied since.
- The women were hired as "computers, S-P 4" — a subprofessional civil
  service grade — through a recruiting drive that specifically advertised
  for women with degrees in mathematics. The Classification Act of 1923
  defines subprofessional work as requiring training "of any degree
  inferior to that represented by graduation from a college." That's on
  the hiring paperwork before any of the credit questions the popular
  version usually raises.
- The famous "100 man-years... completed in two weeks" press line wasn't
  about a firing table. It was the classified Los Alamos hydrogen-bomb
  feasibility run — the first thing actually done on the machine, before
  it did a single trajectory.

The fact card also flags where sources genuinely disagree (tube count,
trajectories per table, setup time) and gives the answer to the four
questions I get asked most on the floor, each with a source. It's built
for a thirty-second answer, not for reading straight through.
