**Suggested thread title:** ENIAC, Part 1 — a source pass that corrects
about a dozen of the standard facts

I put together a four-part series on ENIAC, and Part 1 (just the machine
and the 1946 press rollout — the programmers are Part 2) ended up mostly
being a source-verification exercise. I ran the commonly repeated numbers
back to the 1946 *Report on the ENIAC*, the Maintenance Manual, the BRL
memos and the original War Department press release, and a fair number of
them don't hold up as stated. A sample:

- The "20 hours by hand / 15 minutes on the differential analyzer / 30
  seconds on ENIAC" comparison you've all seen on museum walls is real,
  but it's a 1961 Army capability comparison for a hypothetical trajectory
  — not a wartime measurement. Fine to use as a comparison, not as a
  stopwatch reading.
- "Six firing tables a day" was demanded by the Army is a misquote of "six
  *requests for new tables* a day" — a 1944 BRL memo, with the word
  "requests" dropped somewhere downstream.
- "Eckert ran the tubes at one-quarter power" traces to an uncited
  university history page. The documented figure, from the Maintenance
  Manual, is "at least a two-to-one safety factor" on practically all
  circuits.
- The tube-reliability answer that actually explains how 18,000 vacuum
  tubes worked at all isn't "better tubes" — it's that the circuits used
  tubes purely as on/off switches with signal regeneration at every stage,
  so a weak tube that still switched was a working tube. That's in Adele
  Goldstine's own volume of the 1946 report and gets skipped in most
  popular accounts in favor of "they used good tubes."
- The famous 100-man-year press line wasn't about a firing table — the
  first thing run on ENIAC was a classified Los Alamos feasibility
  calculation, before it ever computed a trajectory.

Full source table with tier ratings (primary/contemporaneous vs. secondary
vs. untraceable) is in the episode's verification pass, and a condensed
one-page version built for quick reference is up as a fact card. Happy to
share the fact card with anyone who wants it regardless of whether the
episode itself is of interest — feedback on the source readings welcome,
especially on the two places sources genuinely conflict (trajectories per
firing table, tube count across machine variants) where I've deliberately
left it unresolved rather than picked a number.

Part 1 covers the machine and the press rollout. Parts 2–4 (the six women
programmers, the EMCC/UNIVAC years, and the 1973 patent trial) are in
progress.
