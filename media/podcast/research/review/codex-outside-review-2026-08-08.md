**1. Factual Smell Test**

- `§4` says the six women worked with "no manuals, no documentation, and no precedent." `No precedent` is fair. `No documentation` is not. They worked from wiring diagrams, block diagrams, engineer explanations, and direct machine access. The real story is hard enough without mythologizing it. Confidence: high.
- `§3` says the Moore School group had been discussing storing instructions in memory "for at least eight months before von Neumann joined." That is too crisp for a murky priority dispute. Early Eckert/Mauchly thinking plainly predates the `First Draft`; the exact timeline and how close it was to a stored-program architecture are not settled enough for that number. Confidence: medium.
- `§3` says Goldstine's circulation of the EDVAC report later helped void the ENIAC patent's novelty claims. Maybe partly, but that is too neat. Honeywell was a multi-ground invalidity decision, not a one-report morality play. Confidence: high.
- `§5.2` says the patent was invalidated on several independent grounds, "each written as sufficient on its own," while the brief also admits nobody read the opinion. That is too legally precise for secondhand sourcing. Confidence: medium-high.
- `§5.2` says two accumulators were operational by July 1944 and the court treated that partial machine as an automatic electronic digital computer. That sounds garbled and is exactly the sort of claim you should not state confidently without the opinion in hand. Confidence: medium.
- `§5.2` quotes `"an exigent afterthought"` from the unseen opinion while `§5.3` warns against quoting uncertain opinion language. That is internally inconsistent. Confidence: high.
- `§2` gives Jennifer Light too much founder credit by calling her 1999 article the start of serious academic attention. Light is important, but that phrasing flattens earlier work and repeats the rediscovery logic the brief elsewhere criticizes. Confidence: medium.
- `§2` says Adele Goldstine "recruited and trained the six women." Training and documentation, yes. `Recruited` sounds too singular and probably overclaimed. Confidence: medium.
- Bibliographic control is sloppier than it should be. Fritz gets only `vol. 18`; `The Computers` gets a mushy `2013-14`; the Adele Goldstine/manual entry is loose on exact title. None of that proves the sources are wrong, but it is where bad history briefs usually start to fray. Confidence: high.

**2. Missing Books**

- Patrick D. McTiernan, `"The ENIAC Patent"` in *IEEE Annals of the History of Computing* (1998). If Part 4 exists, this is not optional.
- William Aspray, *John von Neumann and the Origins of Modern Computing* (MIT Press, 1990). This is the obvious missing serious book for the EDVAC/von Neumann credit fight.
- John von Neumann, *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC* (1945), and Burks/Goldstine/von Neumann, `"Preliminary Discussion of the Logical Design of an Electronic Computing Instrument"` (1946). Dispute A is built around these texts, yet they are not actually listed as sources.
- David A. Grier, *When Computers Were Human* (Princeton, 2005). If you want to explain why six women were there in the first place, this context matters.
- University of Pennsylvania ENIAC/Moore School archival material should be named explicitly. For a podcast, contemporaneous memos, demo materials, and photos are not optional texture; they are scene-building evidence.

**3. Framing Problems**

- `Dispute A`: the brief is trying to be fair, but it quietly installs `distributed authorship` as the neutral center. That is partly right and partly evasive. The settled part is that sole circulation of the `First Draft` under von Neumann's name distorted later credit. The unsettled part is how much of the underlying architecture and stored-program conception should be assigned to whom. The brief is too even-handed about the first point and too settled about the second.
- `Dispute B`: this is the strongest section, but it still adopts a `credit restoration` frame that is narrower than the real issue. The deeper question is not just whether the six women got enough credit; it is why programming was later demoted into clerical or "operator" work in the first place. This is not a balanced controversy anymore. The erasure is real.
- `Dispute C`: this is the least neutral section. It says the `who invented the computer?` question is broken, but then still lets that litigation-shaped question organize the finale. The settled point is that Honeywell invalidated a patent, not that it delivered the final historical verdict on origins. The open point is how much historical weight Larson's derivation finding deserves.

**4. The Part 4 Decision**

- As written, this is half honest and half rationalization, leaning rationalization.
- The honest part is the explicit admission that the primary opinion was not obtained and the refusal to use the famous Atanasoff sentence verbatim.
- The rationalizing part is everything after that: exact finding numbers, exact grounds, exact dates, and even a quoted phrase from the unseen opinion. That is faux precision dressed up as epistemic caution.
- The five guardrails are not sufficient because they regulate wording more than inference. A script could obey all five and still tell listeners, wrongly, that Larson more or less proved Atanasoff was the true inventor of the computer.
- A script could also obey all five and still smuggle in a bad legal lesson: that an unappealed district-court patent case settled the historical question. It settled a patent fight. That is not the same thing.
- Biggest risk: Part 4 can still reproduce the litigation frame while claiming to critique it. If the episode asks `Who Invented the Computer?` and only later says the question is broken, the structure has already conceded too much.

**5. Series Structure**

- The current split is shaky. Part 1 is the driest material, Part 3 is the most overloaded, and Part 4 risks ending the series in abstraction.
- Do not open a two-host podcast with ballistics tables, tube counts, and patch cords. Open with a scene: the 1946 unveiling, the women debugging, the 1952 CBS election call, or the 1973 patent fight. Then explain the machine.
- Part 3 is carrying too much: EMCC, BINAC, Remington Rand, UNIVAC, 1952, IBM, Holberton, Goldstine. Something has to go. BINAC is the easiest cut. Goldstine-to-IBM is expendable unless it directly pays off a later argument.
- Better order: Part 1 should combine the 1946 debut, the six women, and a concise explanation of what ENIAC was. Part 2 should do the wartime build and what programming actually meant. Part 3 should cover EDVAC, the split, EMCC/UNIVAC, and 1952. Part 4 should cover the patent fight and how invention myths get manufactured.
- The `we've been asking the wrong question for four episodes` reveal is structurally cute and intellectually risky. It can feel less like insight than bait-and-switch.
