## 1. The short answer: what to read

If you read **one** book: **Haigh, Priestley & Rope, *ENIAC in Action*** (MIT Press, 2016).
Two of three researchers ranked it #1 independently. It is the current scholarly standard,
covers all four episode threads, and — critically for us — treats the contested claims
historiographically rather than picking a side.

If you read **three**:
1. *ENIAC in Action* — the machine and the historiography
2. Kathy Kleiman, *Proving Ground* (2022) — the six women, built on a decade of interviews
   with four of them before they died
3. Ira Chinoy, *Predicting the Winner* (2024) — UNIVAC and election night 1952

Add **Jean Bartik, *Pioneer Programmer*** (2013) if you want a primary voice. It is the only
memoir by one of the six.

- **Ananyo Bhattacharya, *The Man from the Future*** (Norton, 2022 US / Allen Lane 2021 UK) —
  best current von Neumann biography. ENIAC is a chapter, not the center.
- **Janet Abbate, *Recoding Gender*** (MIT Press, 2012) — the academic counterweight to the

### Tier 3 — popular entry points, read with care

- **Scott McCartney, *ENIAC: The Triumphs and Tragedies***(Walker, 1999) — accessible, uses
  unpublished Eckert/Mauchly correspondence. But he adopts a definition of "computer" that
  conveniently excludes Atanasoff's ABC, which is taking a side while appearing to report.
  Reviewers also questioned his technical precision. **Audiobook available.**
- **Walter Isaacson, *The Innovators*** (Simon & Schuster, 2014) — popularized the women's story
  to a mass audience. Criticized for a reductive men-did-hardware/women-did-software framing
  and for allocating page time by interview access.
- **Claire Evans, *Broad Band*** (Portfolio, 2018) — wide-audience women-in-computing history,
  one ENIAC chapter. Audiobook available.
- **Paul Ceruzzi, *A History of Modern Computing*** (MIT Press, 1998/2003) — connective tissue,
  not ENIAC-specific.
- **Paul Ceruzzi, *Reckoners*** (Greenwood, 1983) — out of print but **free full text online**,
  author-authorized, at ed-thelen.org/comp-hist/Reckoners.html. Positions ENIAC against
  competing relay machines.

### Tier 4 — advocacy literature (useful, but declare the bias)

These are the pro-Atanasoff books. They are worth reading for the trial record, but none is
disinterested:

- **Alice Burks, *Who Invented the Computer?*** (Prometheus, 2003) — fullest trade account of
  the trial, from transcripts. Her husband Arthur Burks was an ENIAC engineer and trial witness.
  A review called it "tendentious" and said it "provides a convincing answer to what is
  fundamentally the wrong question."
- **Burks & Burks, *The First Electronic Computer: The Atanasoff Story*** (U. Michigan, 1988) —
  won a Michigan Press award; criticized for ignoring Colossus and Zuse.
- **Clark Mollenhoff, *Atanasoff: Forgotten Father of the Computer*** (Iowa State UP, 1988) —
  the original advocacy account. Reviewers note "a certain stridency" toward Mauchly.
- **Jane Smiley, *The Man Who Invented the Computer*** (Doubleday, 2010) — novelist's narrative,
  pro-Atanasoff, less legal depth than Burks. Audiobook available.

### Non-book sources worth citing

- **Jennifer S. Light, "When Computers Were Women,"** *Technology and Culture* 40:3 (July 1999),
  455–483. A landmark in academic attention to the programmers' erasure, 15+ years before the
  popular wave. *(Call it a landmark, not the origin — "the article that started it" is the same
  rediscovery logic we're criticizing elsewhere, just moved back a decade.)*
- **W. Barkley Fritz, "The Women of ENIAC,"** *IEEE Annals of the History of Computing*, vol. 18
  (1996). Fritz worked with ENIAC and surveyed the surviving programmers directly.
- **Adele K. Goldstine (1920–1964), *Part I, Technical Description of the ENIAC*** — **CORRECTED
  2026-08-08 from the title pages themselves; see `research/part-1-verification.md` §4f.** *A Report
  on the ENIAC* (Moore School / Army Ordnance, June 1, 1946) is a **five-volume** work, and the
  earlier shorthand here — "she wrote the technical manual" — was wrong in both directions. Per-volume
  title-page credit: **Vol 1, Operating Manual** — Burks and Huskey. **Vol 2, Maintenance Manual** —
  Chu, Cummings, Davis, Huskey, Sharpless, Shaw. **Vols 3–4, Part I, Technical Description** —
  **Adele K. Goldstine, sole credited author.** **Vol 5, Part II** — Huskey. Her name stands alone on
  two of the five volumes exactly as the others' stand on theirs; she is not a "contributor" to a
  corporate work, which is how library catalogues render her. Part I is the volume that explains how
  the machine works without reference to its circuits — Haigh/Priestley/Rope call it "the most
  detailed guide ever produced to the functioning and programming of ENIAC in its original mode."
  She trained the human computers at the Moore School, among them many of ENIAC's first programmers.
  Degrees in mathematics from Hunter College and the University of Michigan; joined the Moore School
  staff 1943. No standalone biography exists; quote her own writing. **All five volumes are free at
  archive.org** — see the verification doc for identifiers.
- **John von Neumann, *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC*** (Moore School, June 1945) — the
  actual document at the center of Dispute A. Widely reproduced. **Read it rather than reading
  about it**; it is short, and the argument is about what's in it.
- **Burks, Goldstine & von Neumann, "Preliminary Discussion of the Logical Design of an
  Electronic Computing Instrument"** (IAS, 1946) — the follow-on, and notably *three* names.
- **University of Pennsylvania / Moore School archives** — contemporaneous memos, demo
  materials, photographs. For a podcast this is scene-building evidence, not footnote texture.
- **Kathy Kleiman, *The Computers*** (documentary, ~20 min, 2014). UNAFF Grand Jury Award 2016.
- **ENIAC Programmers Project** — eniacprogrammers.org
- **Schlesinger Library, Harvard** — holds Kleiman's oral history recordings.

## 3. Dispute A: the stored-program / EDVAC authorship question

**Not seriously disputed:**
- Von Neumann's "First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC" (June 1945) was a genuine and distinct
  intellectual synthesis — the stored-program concept in clean logical form, stripped of
  engineering detail.
- The Moore School group was discussing storing instructions in memory **before** von Neumann
  joined the EDVAC conversations, and the practical engineering thinking was substantially
  theirs. *(Some accounts put this at eight months prior. Don't state a figure on air — the
  timeline is exactly what's murky, and a crisp number implies a precision the record doesn't
  support.)*
- Goldstine circulated the report with von Neumann's name alone on it.
- That open circulation was **among** the disclosures later cited against the ENIAC patent's
  novelty. *(Don't make it the cause. The 1973 invalidation rested on several grounds; "the
  report that killed the patent" is a tidy story and a wrong one.)*

**What's disputed:** the weighting. Eckert and Mauchly held that the report expropriated their
unpublished, commercially sensitive ideas. Von Neumann's defenders hold the formalization was
his alone and engineering antecedents don't diminish it.

**Where each book stands:**
- Goldstine (1972) — von Neumann-centric, written by the man who distributed the report
- Stern (1981) — the corrective, argues for earlier independent Eckert/Mauchly thinking
- Haigh/Priestley/Rope (2016) — treats it historiographically. One reviewer notes it still leans
  Goldstine-adjacent in places, so don't cite it as perfectly neutral.

**Podcast framing:** this is a real disagreement between serious people, not a villain story.
The most defensible position is that authorship was genuinely distributed and the "First Draft"
both overstates von Neumann's sole contribution *and* represents a real synthesis.

## 4. Dispute B: the ENIAC programmers and how credit gets restored

**Not in dispute:** the six women programmed ENIAC with **no precedent** — nobody had programmed
a machine like this because no machine like this existed. They worked from wiring and block
diagrams, engineers' explanations, and direct physical access to the hardware. *(Careful here:
"no documentation" is a myth worth avoiding. There were diagrams and there was Adele Goldstine's
manual. What there wasn't was a programming model, a language, or anyone to ask. The real story
is hard enough.)* They were excluded from the February 1946 unveiling dinner, went unnamed in
photographs, and were written out of early institutional histories that credited "engineers"
over "operators."

**What's overstated:** the claim that nobody knew until a 2010s book rediscovered them. Jennifer
Light's scholarly article ran in 1999. Bartik was publicly advocating for recognition from the
1980s. The popularization is real and valuable but it built on existing academic and activist
work rather than creating the correction from nothing.

**A separate, narrower critique** aimed at Isaacson specifically: his framing (men did hardware,
women did the softer software work) is itself a gendered dichotomy, and his page allocation
tracked interview access more than historical importance.

**Podcast framing:** a genuine historical wrong, substantially corrected over ~25 years of
scholarship and advocacy. That's a *better* episode than heroic-rediscovery — it's a story about
how recognition actually happens, slowly and through many hands.

## 5. Dispute C: the 1973 patent ruling

> **EDITORIAL DECISION (Nick, 2026-08-08):** we are not chasing the primary opinion text.
> Part 4 covers the ruling accurately **at the level of abstraction the format supports** —
> what the court did and what it meant — not paragraph numbers or verbatim quotation.
> The claims below marked *safe at this altitude* are well-supported and can go in a script.
> The items in **§5.3** must be paraphrased, never quoted. Part 4 is **not blocked.**

### What we established

*Safe at this altitude — all of the following is well-supported:*

*Honeywell, Inc. v. Sperry Rand Corp.* (D. Minn.), decided **October 19, 1973**, reported at
**180 U.S.P.Q. 673**, docket 4-67 Civil 138. Judge **Earl R. Larson** held the ENIAC patent
(U.S. 3,120,606, granted 1964) invalid.

**It was invalidated on several independent grounds at once, each written as sufficient on its
own:**

- **Finding 1 — public use.** ENIAC was in use before the June 26, 1946 critical date (filed
  June 26, 1947). Larson fixed Dec 1, 1945 as delivery to Army Ordnance, after which no further
  work counted as "experimental."
- **Finding 2 — on-sale bar.** Construction contract set Dec 31, 1945 delivery; two accumulators
  were operational by July 1944 and the court treated that partial machine as itself an
  automatic electronic digital computer.
- **Finding 7 — prior publication.** Goldstine's June 30, 1945 circulation of the EDVAC First
  Draft, plus Eckert and Mauchly's own Sept 30, 1945 progress report.
- **Finding 3 — derivation from Atanasoff.** The famous one.
- Finding 6 — 3 of 148 claims anticipated by Byron Phelps' 1942 IBM patent (no derivation).
- Finding 10 — 14 of 148 claims invalid via an improper 1963 amendment, which the court
  characterized as a late attempt to capture contributions already in the public domain.
  *(An arresting phrase is attached to this in secondary accounts. It is not reproduced here,
  because we have not seen the opinion — see §5.3, rule 1.)*
- Findings 11 & 13 — allegations of prosecution delay and fraud on the Patent Office were
  **considered and REJECTED**. Loose accounts sometimes report these as findings against
  Eckert and Mauchly. They are not.

**So:** the derivation finding sits in parallel with the other grounds, not subordinate to them.
The "it was really just a technicality" framing is wrong.

**Not appealed.** Sperry Rand, ~$1M into the litigation, chose not to appeal. Consequence: the
ruling settled the dispute and collapsed Sperry's royalty claims industry-wide, but as an
unappealed district court decision it carries **no binding precedential force**.

*(Do not confuse this with* Iowa State University Research Foundation v. Honeywell*, 459 F.2d 447
(8th Cir. 1972) — a pretrial appeal about intervention, decided before the merits ruling.)*

### §5.3 — Guardrails: five things the script must NOT do

The primary opinion text could not be retrieved (CourtListener, Justia, Casetext, Google Scholar,
and the Wayback copy were all blocked, missing, or dead). The Finding numbers above come from a
well-cited secondary compilation, corroborated by accounts that may share an upstream source.
That is fine for the abstraction we're working at — but it draws five hard lines:

1. **Never quote the Atanasoff sentence verbatim.** It circulates in two versions: *"did not
   themselves invent the automatic electronic computer"* and *"did not themselves **first**
   invent the automatic electronic **digital** computer."* Those are different claims and we
   don't know which is real. **Paraphrase always:** "Larson found that Eckert and Mauchly had
   derived the core ideas from Atanasoff rather than originating them." That's the substance
   both versions agree on.
2. **Don't cite a statute.** Whether the opinion invokes 35 U.S.C. §102(f) is unconfirmed. Say
   "derivation" in plain English; don't reach for a section number.
3. **Don't say "the court concluded as a matter of law."** Whether derivation sits in Findings of
   Fact or Conclusions of Law is unconfirmed. "The court found" is accurate and safe.
4. **Don't cite Finding numbers on air.** They're secondhand, and no listener needs them.
5. **Attribute the antitrust reading, don't assert it.** "Some historians read the ruling as
   aimed at breaking Sperry Rand's grip on the industry" — not "historians agree it was really
   about antitrust." That framing is plausible but wasn't pinned to a named source.

6. **The two inferences the script must not leave in a listener's head.** The five rules above
   govern *wording*. This one governs what the audience walks away believing, which is where the
   real risk is — a script can obey rules 1–5 perfectly and still land both of these:
   - **"A court proved Atanasoff invented the computer."** It did not. It invalidated a patent.
     A finding that Eckert and Mauchly derived ideas from Atanasoff is not a finding that
     Atanasoff built the first computer, and the ABC was a special-purpose machine that never
     ran fully. If a listener finishes Part 4 believing a judge settled the invention question,
     the episode failed regardless of how carefully each sentence was phrased.
   - **"The trial settled the history."** It settled a patent fight between two companies. An
     unappealed district-court ruling is a commercial outcome, not a historical verdict. Say
     this out loud in the episode rather than trusting it to be implied.

**"Never appealed"** is repeated by every secondary source and traced to a docket by none. It's
almost certainly true and it's safe to say at this altitude; if you want to be scrupulous,
"Sperry Rand didn't take it up on appeal" carries the same meaning with less forensic weight.

### Script-safe language

> "In 1973, federal judge Earl Larson threw out the ENIAC patent — not on one ground, but on
> several independent ones at once. The machine had been publicly demonstrated and effectively
> on sale more than a year before the patent was filed, key details had already been published,
> and separately, Larson found that Eckert and Mauchly hadn't invented the automatic electronic
> digital computer themselves but had derived core ideas from Dr. John Vincent Atanasoff. That
> derivation finding wasn't a throwaway aside — it was one of the court's actual stated bases
> for invalidity. Sperry Rand didn't appeal, so it was never tested by a higher court. It settled
> the patent dispute for good, but it never became binding precedent, and historians still argue
> about how much weight one trial's factual findings should carry in the bigger question."

That paragraph is defensible on the current evidence. It avoids verbatim quotation, avoids
asserting the statutory basis, and states the non-appeal without claiming docket confirmation.

## 6. Proposed series structure

> **Two structural notes from the outside review, both adopted:**
> **(a) Don't open cold on hardware.** Ballistics tables and tube counts are the driest material
> in the series and they're currently the first thing a listener hears. Every episode — Part 1
> especially — opens on a *scene*, then earns the exposition. Candidate cold opens: the February
> 1946 unveiling; a programmer tracing a fault to a single dead tube among eighteen thousand;
> CBS refusing to believe its own machine on election night.
> **(b) Part 4's title concedes the frame it means to attack.** Asking "Who Invented the
> Computer?" for 30 minutes and then revealing the question is broken is a bait-and-switch, and
> it hands the litigation-shaped framing a full episode of oxygen first. Retitled below. The
> hosts should name the problem with the question early and spend the episode on the more
> interesting one: *how invention myths get manufactured, and who benefits.*

**Part 1 — The Machine.** Moore School 1943–45. Ballistics tables as the forcing function.
18,000 vacuum tubes and what that meant physically. Programming by patch cord.
*Sources:* ENIAC in Action; Rojas & Hashagen for architecture; Reckoners for context.

**Part 2 — The Programmers.** The six women. What programming meant with no manual. The 1946
unveiling. Then the slow 25-year restoration of credit — Light 1999, Bartik's own advocacy,
Kleiman, Isaacson — and what that says about how history gets corrected.
*Sources:* Proving Ground; Pioneer Programmer; Light 1999; Abbate; Fritz.

**Part 3 — What Came Next.** Eckert-Mauchly Computer Corp, undercapitalization, the sale to
Remington Rand. UNIVAC I. Election night 1952 — the machine called it right and CBS didn't
believe it. Then IBM eats the market. Holberton to COBOL.
*Sources:* Chinoy (centerpiece); Stern; Flamm; Pugh; Beyer.
*Overloaded — the outside review flagged this episode as carrying the most and it's right.*
**Cut BINAC** to a sentence; it's a stepping stone, not a story. **Cut Goldstine-to-IBM** unless
it pays off something later. If it's still bursting at script stage, election night can carry
the whole second half and IBM's win becomes the closing beat rather than a section.

**Part 4 — How to Manufacture an Inventor.** The EDVAC report. The patent. The 1973 trial. Not
"who invented the computer" — *why we keep asking a question that a patent fight invented, and
who gains from each available answer.* The court's own frame is the episode's subject, not its
scaffolding.
*Sources:* ENIAC in Action; McTiernan (get it); Burks (declared as advocacy); §5 above.
**Observe all six §5.3 guardrails** — especially rule 6, which governs what the listener
believes at the end rather than what the hosts said.
