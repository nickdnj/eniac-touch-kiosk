# A Signal Corps radar job pushed Mauchly toward ENIAC

**There is one documented link between the Signal Corps's New Jersey laboratories and ENIAC, and it is a motive, not a contribution; that the lab was Camp Evans is an inference.** In his 10 January 1973 Smithsonian oral history, John Mauchly says the Moore School obtained a classified contract from "the Signal Corps in New Jersey" to compute radar-antenna radiation patterns, and that the slow desk-calculator arithmetic on that job impelled him back toward an electronic computer ([Smithsonian NMAH AC0196](https://www.si.edu/media/NMAH/NMAH-AC0196_mauc730110.pdf)). The transcript never names Camp Evans, Evans Signal Laboratory, Fort Monmouth or Belmar, and the project owner's team confirms the 6 February 1973 session (21 pp.) contains none of those names either. The "Camp Evans" label is InfoAge's, resting on that one recollection and no archival document located in this research. ENIAC's own money was Army Ordnance money, contract W-670-ORD-4926, supervised by the Ballistic Research Laboratory ([Army Ordnance history](https://ed-thelen.org/comp-hist/U-S-Ord-61-ch02.html)); no Signal Corps funding is documented. Of the six claims on InfoAge's Computer Research page, only "helped inspire the ENIAC developers" has a documented core, and even that core is Mauchly's motivation on a Moore School job, not work at Camp Evans. The remaining claims range from plausible but unproven (1941-1957 campus computer research; MOBIDIC) to unsupported as worded ("first computers with output screens"; "directly led to ASCII"). The post-ENIAC Signal Corps computing story is real, but it ran mostly from the Fort Monmouth main post and was built largely by contractors. Caveat on reach: the five research streams could not access Stern, Goldstine, Burks, McCartney, Haigh/Priestley/Rope, the full Brainerd 1976 article, or Penn Archives files, so every negative below means "not found in what could be read," not "absent from the record."

## The one primary source says "New Jersey" and nothing more

Mauchly's account is the root of everything. He says Dr. Brainerd negotiated with the Signal Corps in New Jersey a contract studying the radiation pattern of a parabolic reflector with a dipole at its focus, aimed at sharper radar target definition, solved by step-by-step numerical integration. The contract was partly experimental: colleagues built and tested a physical reflector while Mauchly's group computed. He admits he never really saw the contract, cannot fix its dates, and says the Signal Corps cared only about its results ([Smithsonian NMAH AC0196](https://www.si.edu/media/NMAH/NMAH-AC0196_mauc730110.pdf)). He had not finished the patterns when ENIAC work began and completed them concurrently in early 1943. Text searches of the full transcript for Evans, Monmouth, Belmar, Red Bank and Camp returned nothing.

The only other participant voice reached is Brainerd's 1976 article, quoted second-hand: it lists "project PZ," a Signal Corps project Brainerd obtained, on which Burks and Mauchly did related radar work, separate from "project PL," the Eckert/Chedaker/Sharpless circuit and delay-line work ([arXiv 1908.09549](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.09549)). The "PZ" label is unverified: I did not see the original (Technology and Culture 17(3), 1976, pp. 482-488), and the Oprema paper's authors caution that Brainerd is unreliable on another point. PZ is probably a Moore School internal project code, parallel to PL, not a Signal Corps contract number; that is inference. Nothing found names the Signal Corps laboratory, the contracting office, a technical monitor, a contract number or a dollar amount.

Which Signal Corps body was it? In 1941-43 the Signal Corps's New Jersey research establishment was centred on Fort Monmouth (Oceanport/Eatontown), the main post and home of the Signal Corps Laboratories headquarters, while Camp Evans (Wall/Belmar) was a separate radar-oriented outstation. Mauchly's words fit either, or a procurement or engineering office. Radar work being concentrated at Evans makes it plausible; nothing demonstrates it. Sources also disagree on when the site became "Evans Signal Laboratory" (1941-42 versus April 1945, per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_Corps_Laboratories)), so even the site's name at the time of the contract is unsettled.

## Mauchly's people are documented; any Camp Evans people are not

The documented personnel link runs through the Moore School, not through New Jersey. Mauchly and Brainerd ran the job; Burks was assigned and stayed in touch with Mauchly's computing; Sharpless was on the experimental end; Witmer helped; and Mauchly hired desk-calculator "computers," one of whom he names as Marlene Wescott ([Smithsonian NMAH AC0196](https://www.si.edu/media/NMAH/NMAH-AC0196_mauc730110.pdf)). Burks and Sharpless then moved onto ENIAC when the contract allowed, so the Signal Corps job was a feeder of staff, but a Moore School one. Earlier research in this project concluded that Wescott is very likely Marlyn Wescoff (later Meltzer), one of the six ENIAC programmers, on several matched details; that finding is unproven, and her own accounts describe her first job as weather calculations, which has not been reconciled with Mauchly's antenna job. A kiosk can say Mauchly recalled hiring a woman matching Wescoff on the antenna contract; it should not state flatly that she did.

On the Signal Corps side, no officer, scientist or monitor is named by anyone reached. The searches for Zahl, Colton, DeWitt, Blair, Ziegler, Clark and others visiting, reviewing or requesting time on ENIAC found nothing. Harold Zahl, often linked with Fort Monmouth research, is shown by InfoAge's own biography as research director from 1948, after the ENIAC period ([InfoAge Zahl biography](https://www.infoage.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/014_2007_harold_zahl.pdf)). No ENIAC engineer is documented as moving to or from Camp Evans. A single 2011 list post has a man in his late 80s saying he built a tube computer at Camp Evans after the war; it is unsourced and has no ENIAC tie ([SIGCIS thread](https://lists.sigcis.org/archives/list/members@lists.sigcis.org/thread/4Z4TGLWORM2BI55FYIDKQJ5F4BUHEGBV/)). No Signal Corps participant is named in any account of the February 1946 dedication that was reached, and no Signal Corps problem on ENIAC itself was found. Evan Koblentz's 2011 claim on that same list that Camp Evans was a customer of the Moore School differential analyzer in 1947 is unsourced and unanswered.

## Contracts, money and technology point to Ordnance, the Rad Lab and Moore School

ENIAC's contract was dated 5 June 1943, started at **$61,700**, and grew through nine supplements to **$486,804.22**, with technical supervision under the Ballistic Research Laboratory at Aberdeen ([Army Ordnance history](https://ed-thelen.org/comp-hist/U-S-Ord-61-ch02.html)). The Moore School had operated the differential analyzer for the Army since June 1942. The Ordnance history does not mention the Signal Corps, radar, Camp Evans or OSRD. The only Signal Corps administrative touch in Mauchly's account is that the Signal Corps contract helped the school secure a priority for extra desk calculators. The Signal Corps contract preceded ENIAC, shared faculty with it, and was separate from it in money.

On technology, the documented lineage of Eckert's pulse and delay-line work is Moore School radar-timing work associated with MIT's Radiation Laboratory, not Camp Evans, and delay lines were an EDVAC-era memory, not an ENIAC circuit ([Wikipedia, Delay-line memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delay-line_memory); NAE tribute summaries, unverified). Mauchly describes ENIAC's counter circuits as Eckert's survey and reliability testing of published circuits. Parabolic-antenna research at Evans, Project Diana, the SCR-270/584 radars: none is tied to the Moore School in any source. The honest classification is that the antenna job supplied a computational burden, a classified-work track record for the Moore School, and staff, but no hardware or technique.

## The six InfoAge claims, one by one

Everything below comes from the Computer Research page itself, which carries no citations or author ([InfoAge Computer Research](https://www.infoage.org/history-ia/computer-research/)), plus the Koblentz article ([InfoAge](https://infoage.org/eniac-development-impelled-by-camp-evans-project/)) and a VCF page ([VCF](https://vcfed.org/?p=291)). Note: the research streams disagree on whether the Koblentz article's body says "Camp Evans"; one fetch summary says it does not, another quotes it saying so; check the live page.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Campus in computer research "from about 1941-1957" | A Signal Corps radar job in NJ motivated Mauchly (1941-43); Fieldata ran 1955-62 and MOBIDIC was contracted 1956, both managed from Fort Monmouth | Evans staff took part in some of that work | Any computer-research programme at Camp Evans as such; no anchor for 1957 beyond a VCF "Micro-Module" mention | InfoAge, undated, unsourced |

The ENIAC-adjacent claims therefore stand or fall on one recollection plus an inferred site. The 1941 start matches Mauchly's antenna period or the Army's acquisition of the camp (per the NHL nomination, inference); no source anchors 1957. The Army's own article on early mobile computing places Fieldata and MOBIDIC under the Signal Research and Development Laboratory at Fort Monmouth ([U.S. Army CECOM](https://www.army.mil/article/291122/early_army_mobile_computing_development_in_the_signal_corps)) and mentions neither Camp Evans nor Sylvania. The Jennings character-code history credits Fieldata with large influence on ASCII yet notes ASCII's design was far along when Fieldata arrived ([sr-ix.com](https://www.sr-ix.com/Archive/CharCodeHist/)); Mackenzie's 1980 book, the standard reference, was located but not read.

## After ENIAC the Signal Corps built computers, but through Fort Monmouth and contractors

Nothing found shows that Fort Monmouth or Camp Evans operated ENIAC, EDVAC, ORDVAC or a UNIVAC in 1946-57, or that an ENIAC alumni roster existed there; SCEL annual reports, the Monmouth Message and the Charles Babbage Institute were not searched. The real post-ENIAC thread is the Moore School's **MSAC**, a machine built for the Army Signal Corps from about 1950-51 and stopped near 85 percent complete in November 1953 ([Hagley](https://findingaids.hagley.org/repositories/3/resources/1853); [Penn Archives](https://archives.upenn.edu/?p=2646)); sponsorship is conflicted, since a 1950 ONR bulletin cited via search says SCEL sponsored component research while Ordnance was the sponsor. That gives the Signal Corps's Moore School relationship a second documented chapter, still at Fort Monmouth's level and not at Evans. Brainerd later supervised a Signal Corps data-retrieval project ([ETHW](https://ethw.org/John_G._Brainerd), unverified). Fieldata (1955-62) set code, instruction-set and interface standards across computers, and MOBIDIC was its showpiece; Sylvania built it in Needham, Massachusetts. "Camp Evans engineers designed it" is, at best, a claim about which Signal Corps staff supervised or advised, and no document reached demonstrates even that.

## Safe wording for the kiosk or the museum

Safe, documented: "In the early 1940s the Moore School took on a classified Signal Corps contract to calculate radar-antenna radiation patterns. Hand calculation was slow; John Mauchly later recalled that this work pushed him back toward the idea of an electronic computer. ENIAC itself was paid for by the Army Ordnance Department." Safe attribution of the location: "a Signal Corps laboratory in New Jersey." Safe on people: "Several of the people on that antenna contract, including Mauchly, Burks and Sharpless, went on to build ENIAC." On the post-ENIAC story: "In the 1950s the Signal Corps, from Fort Monmouth, ran Fieldata and contracted MOBIDIC, a trailer-mounted computer built by Sylvania; Fieldata's character code was one of the influences on ASCII."

Say with attribution only: "InfoAge, which operates the former Camp Evans, believes the Signal Corps client was Evans Signal Laboratory; no surviving document located so far names the laboratory." Do not say: that Camp Evans funded, contributed technology to, or supplied people to ENIAC; that it "inspired" the developers (as opposed to a Signal Corps contract motivating Mauchly); that it ran computer research 1941-57; that it lent technology to the first computers with screens; that it designed the first non-prototype mobile computers; that it "directly led to" ASCII; that the contract was called "Project PZ"; or that Camp Evans and Fort Monmouth's main post are one place. Keep them separate at every mention. Do not state flatly that Marlyn Wescoff worked on the antenna contract.

## Next steps to close the gap

1. Penn University Archives and Records Center, Moore School files (contract folders, annual and progress reports, correspondence for 1941-43): most likely to name the Signal Corps office, a contract number and the PZ code in one stroke.
2. National Archives RG 111 (Signal Corps) and Army Ordnance contract records for the Moore School, plus Fort Monmouth/Evans Signal Laboratory records and annual reports (CECOM historian; Camp Evans collection at InfoAge): would show whether a Moore School task order was ever issued by Evans.
3. Brainerd, "Genesis of the ENIAC," Technology and Culture 17(3), 1976, pp. 482-488 (via JSTOR or a library): verify the PZ passage and its wording, a cheap, quick check.
4. Burks and Burks 1981 (Annals of the History of Computing), Stern's *From ENIAC to UNIVAC*, Goldstine, McCartney, and Haigh/Priestley/Rope's *ENIAC in Action*, searched for Signal Corps, Evans and Monmouth: the standard literature has not yet been checked at all.
5. Other Smithsonian AC0196 transcripts (Brainerd, Sharpless, Burks, Eckert, Goldstine) and the Stern 1977 Mauchly interview catalogued by AIP ([AIP](https://history.aip.org/catalog/icos/31773.html)): a second participant naming the lab would settle it.
6. Ask Koblentz/InfoAge directly what document supports the Camp Evans identification, and the 1947 differential-analyzer and 1953 CRT claims; also the earlier SIGCIS explanation he refers to, and the Bill Mauchly lectures (Trenton 1977, VCF East 5.0).
7. Mackenzie's *Coded Character Sets* and the ASA X3.2 committee records, to bound Fieldata's ASCII role; Whirlwind/SAGE records to test the CRT claim; DTIC MSAC report AD0694599 (blocked here) for MSAC sponsorship.
8. Wescoff's own interviews (Fritz 1996 and others), for what her first assignment was.

## Conclusion

The question is narrower than the public claim. The documented fact, a Signal Corps radar-antenna contract that burdened Mauchly with hand calculation, is a genuine and well-attested origin story for the ENIAC proposal, and it involves the Signal Corps in New Jersey. What InfoAge adds, that the lab was Camp Evans and that Evans technology inspired or shaped ENIAC, is an identification, not a finding, and the later-era claims shade from Fort Monmouth programmes onto Camp Evans without support.

What this changes for the exhibit is the direction of the arrow: the documented traffic ran from a Signal Corps client to the Moore School as a problem, not from a Signal Corps laboratory to ENIAC as a technology. The gap is closable, because the likely answer sits in a handful of archived contract files. Until then the kiosk can tell the real story fully without leaning on the unproven site name.
