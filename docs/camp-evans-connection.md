# Camp Evans and ENIAC: what the sources actually say

**Date:** 2026-10-15
**Author:** Nick DeMarco with AI assistance
**Status:** Research note. Not reviewed by Doug Crawford or the Fact-Checker. Nothing here is in the kiosk yet.
**Question (Nick):** InfoAge's "Computer Research" page says its campus did computer research that "helped inspire the ENIAC developers". How does that really connect to ENIAC?

## 1. Short answer

There is a real, documented link, but it is smaller and different from the headline.

- **Documented (Mauchly, 1973):** while John Mauchly was on the Moore School faculty (1941 onward), the school took a **Signal Corps contract in New Jersey** to calculate how a radar antenna's radiation pattern changes with the shape of its dish. It was done by hand on desk calculators. He says the slog kept pushing him back to his idea of an electronic computer, and that people who worked on that contract then moved onto ENIAC.
- **Not documented:** that the contract was with **Camp Evans** specifically. Neither of the two Mauchly transcripts the museum article cites names Camp Evans, Fort Monmouth or Belmar. They say "the Signal Corps in New Jersey". Camp Evans was a Signal Corps radar laboratory in New Jersey, so it is a reasonable inference, but it is an inference until a contract record or a second source says so.
- **Overstated by the headline:** Mauchly says the antenna project **did not convince anyone**, and that the thing which made an electronic computer look like the answer was the Army's Ballistics Research Laboratory falling behind on firing tables. That is also the story our Trajectory mission tells.

## 2. What InfoAge says

- Page: https://www.infoage.org/history-ia/computer-research/. It says the campus was part of computer research "from about 1941-1957", that its projects "helped inspire the ENIAC developers", and that they led to ASCII and early mobile computers. It names no people, projects or documents for any of those claims.
- Article it links: "ENIAC development 'impelled' by Camp Evans project", by Evan Koblentz, https://infoage.org/eniac-development-impelled-by-camp-evans-project/. It argues the antenna research was "part of the reason" ENIAC happened, resting on two Smithsonian oral-history transcripts of Mauchly. No publication date is shown.

## 3. The primary source, checked

Smithsonian, Computer Oral History Collection, John W. Mauchly interviewed by Henry Tropp, 10 January 1973 (72 pp.): https://www.si.edu/media/NMAH/NMAH-AC0196_mauc730110.pdf. The 6 February 1973 session (21 pp.) is https://www.si.edu/media/NMAH/NMAH-AC0196_mauc730206.pdf and has nothing on this topic. Smithsonian permits quotation under fair use with citation. Page numbers below are the PDF page of the 10 January transcript.

| What the article claims | What the transcript says | Page |
|---|---|---|
| A Signal Corps contract needed antenna-pattern calculations | Confirmed. Grist Brainerd arranged a contract "with the Signal Corps in New Jersey" to study a parabolic reflector with a dipole at its focus. Classified at the time. The method was numerical integration. | 15 |
| Moore School had almost no calculators; Mauchly borrowed machines | Confirmed. The school had one desk calculator; he got Ursinus's Monroe, and the school got one more through a priority request. (The article's "10-12 machines borrowed from Wharton" is from a passage I did not locate in this transcript; it may be in the 6 February session or a third part.) | 18 |
| The project ran over time and the Signal Corps only wanted results | Confirmed. It overran the promised period; the sponsor was content with the results and the school got a follow-on. | 19-20 |
| Mauchly was "impelled" toward an electronic computer by the slog | Confirmed in his words: he says he was "impelled, you might say, to come back to that subject" each time he was fed up with the hand calculations on the Signal Corps project. | 20 |
| Camp Evans specifically | **Not in the transcript.** The word does not appear in either transcript. | none |
| The stronger driver was the Army ballistics need | Mauchly says the only real hope was understanding the Ballistics Research Laboratory's needs, because it was falling behind on firing tables; about a hundred women with desk calculators in 1943 could not keep up. | 20 |

## 4. How it really connects (documented, in Mauchly's words)

1. **A motive.** The antenna contract made desk-calculator drudgery concrete for Mauchly, and he returned to the electronic-computer idea whenever it got to him (p. 20). It sat beside, not in place of, the ballistics need.
2. **A timeline overlap.** He was still finishing the antenna radiation-pattern calculations after the ENIAC project started (p. 33).
3. **People.** T. Kite Sharpless, who was on the experimental side of the Signal Corps project, was put onto ENIAC whenever the contract work freed him (pp. 34, 48). Arthur Burks "had been on the Signal Corps project" too (p. 48). Both are on our honor roll's "project team" side.
4. **A lead worth checking.** Mauchly says he hired "Marlene Wescott" as one of his computers on the Signal Corps project, a history major who needed no mathematics to follow the tabular steps (p. 69). That is very probably Marlyn Wescoff, one of ENIAC's first six programmers, whom the kiosk honours. But the transcript's spelling differs, and it says her husband was a dentist named Phil, which our own record does not confirm. **Verify before using it.**
5. **Who paid for ENIAC.** The Signal Corps contract is a separate contract. ENIAC's own contract came through the Army's Ordnance Department and the Ballistics Research Laboratory (see `docs/trajectory-history.md` and the 1946 *Report on the ENIAC*).

## 5. What is not supported by anything I found

- "Directly led to ASCII", "loaned technology to the first computers with output screens" and "earliest non-prototype mobile computers" on the InfoAge page: no names, projects or sources. Not checked here, not repeatable as fact.
- The date range 1941-1957 for "computer research" at the campus: unsourced on the page.
- Any claim that work done on the Camp Evans site itself designed or built part of ENIAC. Nothing in Mauchly's account says so.

## 6. Safe wording, if the kiosk or video ever mentions it

Say what Mauchly said, and keep the place at the level the source gives:

> Before ENIAC, John Mauchly and his Moore School colleagues spent months working out radar antenna patterns for the Army Signal Corps in New Jersey, by hand, on desk calculators. The slog was one of the things that kept pushing him toward an electronic computer. [FC]

Add "Camp Evans" only after a source names it. Do not write "Camp Evans inspired ENIAC" or "ENIAC was built because of".
