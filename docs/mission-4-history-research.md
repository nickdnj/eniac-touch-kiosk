# Mission 4 (Nim) history fact-check: Nimatron, Nimrod, Nim, and what ENIAC never did

**Most of the "early game machine" story is safe to show, with four corrections.** The Nimatron (Westinghouse, 1940) and its patent are verified against the patent itself; the Nimrod (Ferranti, 1951) is verified against the Science Museum Group record and the Ferranti booklet, with its debut date conflicting across sources. Bouton's 1901 paper has been read: he named the game, and his Nim is a *three-pile* game, so a one-pile take-1-to-3 game should be labelled a "one-pile Nim variant" or "subtraction game," not simply "Nim." The corrections: (1) the "90% win rate" is a secondary figure, and the losses were mostly staged by attendants; (2) "first computer built to play a game" for Nimrod is contested, since Nimatron (1940) and Bertie the Brain (1950) predate it; (3) the Ludwig Erhard story is a press anecdote with no cited primary source; (4) no credible source says ENIAC ever played a game, so any copy that implies it did is wrong. Finally, a machine that moves second in a multiple-of-4 subtraction game cannot lose by arithmetic alone, so "Can you beat ENIAC?" needs honest framing. Two sources could not be opened (Condon's 1942 article and his 1968 oral history; both paywalled or 403), so those rows are marked [S].

Confidence tags: [V] verified against a primary or scholarly source; [S] secondary source only; [C] conflicting sources; [U] unverified.

## Notes

**Nimatron.** The hard facts are the patent's: Westinghouse, three named inventors, filed 26 April 1940, granted 24 September 1940. Everything about attendance and win rate traces to Condon himself (1942 note, 1968 interview), which this research could not open; the "90%" is a division nobody at the time stated, and Condon reportedly said the machine's dozen fixed patterns let attendants beat it at will. Safe: "played about 100,000 games at the fair and won most of them; most of its losses were to Westinghouse attendants showing it could be beaten." Avoid a bare "won 90%."

**Nimrod.** Call it "an early special-purpose electronic computer built to play Nim." Do not call it "the first computer built to play a game": Nimatron did so with relays in 1940, and Bertie the Brain did so with tubes in 1950; CHM itself flags the earlier machine. Use "May 1951" for the London debut and "6–21 October 1951" for Berlin. The Erhard story appears in a 2001 heise.de column with no source and is repeated by the HNF museum blog citing heise; if used, write "German press accounts say the economics minister Ludwig Erhard lost three games to it."

**Nim vs subtraction game.** Bouton's Nim has several piles and allows taking any number from one pile; the optimal strategy is binary (XOR). A single pile with a take-limit of three is a different, far simpler game, properly a *subtraction game*, and Bouton never analysed it. Safe visitor wording: "a one-pile version of the ancient game Nim" or "a Nim-style counting game"; add "the full game, with several piles, was solved by Harvard's Charles Bouton in 1901." Do not say "Bouton proved the multiple-of-4 rule."

**ENIAC.** No credible source, scholarly or popular, has ENIAC playing any game, and its documented workload is numerical. Do not borrow Bertie the Brain (Toronto, 1950, tic-tac-toe, lights), OXO (Cambridge, 1952, EDSAC, CRT), Strachey's draughts or Prinz's chess (both Ferranti Mark 1, 1951–52) for ENIAC. The jeredw "eniac-chess" repository is a modern reconstruction, not history.

**Honest framing for the mission.** "Can you beat ENIAC?" is fine as a game title if the copy makes clear the kiosk is a simulation. "ENIAC always wins" is misleading twice over: ENIAC never played, and the unbeatable position belongs to whoever moves second from a multiple of 4. Suggested wording: "In this game the player who always leaves a multiple of four cannot lose. Our ENIAC knows that rule. Can you find it and turn it against the machine?" If the design wants beatable play, let the visitor choose who goes first, or start from a non-multiple of 4 when the machine moves second.
